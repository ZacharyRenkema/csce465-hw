Clarifying a key security term in the assignment wording.
Careful — the assignment says principals, not principles. Different words, and Chapter 1 of van Oorschot covers both, which is where the confusion usually comes from.

Principal is the security term of art. A principal is an entity that can be identified and to which privileges are assigned — the "who" that an action is attributed to and on whose authority it happens. Users, processes running under a particular account, services, roles. The defining test: can you name a distinct set of permissions this entity holds, and can an action be attributed to it? If yes, it's a principal.

Distinguishing it from the other two tables you're filling:

An asset is a thing with value that you're protecting. Passive.
A principal is an actor that holds privileges. Active.
A trust boundary is the line between principals with different privilege levels, where data crossing changes how much it should be trusted.
Where to look in your diagram: for each box, ask whose authority its actions run under. Some boxes on the diagram are one principal; some collapse two; some are just data stores and aren't principals at all. A useful discriminator is the OS account — a process running as your VM user has exactly that user's filesystem privileges, and anything it invokes inherits them.

The reason this matters for your Task 3 writeup: prompt injection is best described as a confused deputy problem. A deputy holds privileges legitimately, on behalf of one principal, and is tricked into exercising them on behalf of another. If you've named your principals precisely, you can say exactly whose privileges ran the marker script and whose intent it served — and note those are not the same principal. That's a much stronger claim than "the model got tricked," and it's the kind of evidence-over-labels answer the assignment is asking for.

Separately, the textbook's principles (least privilege, complete mediation, fail-safe defaults, and so on) aren't required by Task 4's label list — but they're the natural vocabulary for your control column in the threats table, and naming the principle a control implements tends to read well.
